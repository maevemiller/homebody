# How designers run intake and discovery

Last updated: 2026-10-27. Part of the Homebody interior-design reference (see `README.md`).

This is how professional designers and e-design services get from "a stranger with a room" to "a brief I can
design from". It informs the order and tone of the Taste Builder.

---

## 1. The shape of a professional intake

Across designer blogs, trade press and practice-management tools, the intake has three beats. They're the same
whether the designer is a full-service firm or a $159 online service.

| Beat | What happens | What the designer gets |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Connect** | A short call or meeting, often at the home. No heavy form first. | Fit, rapport, a sense of who decides, the "why now". |
| **2. Questionnaire** | A form or quiz, broad to narrow: life and routines first, then priorities, then taste and feeling. Images for style. | Facts, constraints, a first read on taste. |
| **3. Discovery conversation** | A follow-up call to clarify vague or conflicting answers, react to images together, and hear what a form can't capture. | The real brief: what "done" looks like, what they'd never live with, the emotional goal. |

It closes with a **summary read back to the client** (see `dossier.md`): "Here's what I heard." That moment is
where trust is built and errors get caught.

Designers Today argues that a heavy form before any human contact is backward: busy prospects won't fill it out, and
forms only capture surface preferences. Its author's firm runs the questionnaire as a funnel (lifestyle and family
first, then design priorities and "emotional nuance"), then a required follow-up call. The call is where one partner
speaks up who stayed quiet on the form, and where polite written answers become honest ones.

**For Homebody:** the conversation *is* beat 3, so we can run it like the call rather than the form. Space Capture
already did part of beat 2 (photos, measurements, keepers).

## 2. The order of topics

Designers broadly agree on this order. It's also the order Maeve asked for.

1. **Why now, and what's the project.** "What made you want to do this now?" "What isn't working?" The trigger
   (a move, a baby, a breakup, working from home) frames everything else.
2. **Function and life.** Who lives here, what happens in the room on an ordinary day and on an occasional one
   (guests, holidays), kids, pets, work, storage, light, noise. This decides the furniture list before style does.
3. **Aesthetics and feeling.** How they want the room to *feel*; images they react to; colors, materials, patterns;
   what they'd never live with.
4. **Personal story.** Where they've lived, places that felt like home, objects with meaning, heritage. Designers
   tend to save this for when rapport exists; it's what makes the room theirs rather than a catalog.
5. **Practical constraints.** Budget, timeline, what stays, renting vs owning, decision-makers. Many designers ask
   budget *after* hearing the vision, so they can set honest expectations against it (Cyncly); others put it up
   front to qualify the lead. For a product, ask it as a range, after the client has invested a little.
6. **Close.** "What would make this a success for you?" (101 Studio notes the answer often reveals the real goal.)
   Then a short summary read back.

## 3. How the e-design services do it

| Service | Intake | Style capture | What you get back |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Havenly** | ~5 min free quiz → matched to a designer → room profile (photos from every angle incl. the parts you dislike, measurements, budget, "keepers") → **Design Discovery call** | React to room photos: rooms you like and rooms you'd "never live in". No design vocabulary needed. Results pick your designer. | Concept board, 3D render, to-scale floor plan, priced shopping list, **designer notes explaining the reasoning**; revisions. |
| **Modsy** (closed 2022; archived) | Pick room + *why* you're redesigning (moving, replacing a few pieces) → how far along the room is (scratch → final touches) → keepers with photos → uses (kids, pets), special requests (storage, a focal point like a fireplace), budget | Several rounds of room photos; multi-select; optional Pinterest upload. Top picks became a mood board you could like/dislike item by item. Results were named blends, e.g. "Rustic Warmth" = rustic + transitional + a hint of mid-century. | 3D renders with close-ups, designer notes on layout. |
| **Laurel & Wolf** (closed 2020) | Short quiz "simple images and word association" → photos + dimensions → **design brief**: the space, how you'll live in it, needs, budget per room | Images + word association | 3–6 "First Looks" boards from competing designers, then final style boards, floor plan, set-up instructions, shopping list. |
| **Decorist** | ~5 min visual "style profile" quiz (aesthetic, space, budget; e.g. pick a favorite sofa, even a celebrity's style) → matched with a designer | Visual picks | Concepts and feedback rounds with the designer until it's right. |
| **Generic designer questionnaires** (Arch-style, Jotform/Tally/Bonsai templates) | Household (who, ages, pets, work, hobbies), room function, style and colour likes/dislikes, materials, budget bands, schedule, special requirements; photos of every wall plus ceiling height, door and window heights. | Usually a list of style names to tick (Country, Modern, Art Deco…), plus favorite and disliked colors. | A brief for the designer. |

Patterns worth copying:
- **Show, don't name.** Every successful service captures style through images, never by asking for a label.
- **Dislikes are as valuable as likes.** Havenly's "rooms you'd never live in"; Cyncly's "what do you dislike about
  the current design"; 101 Studio asks for 10–15 liked and 5 disliked images.
- **"Why are you redesigning?" and "how far along is it?"** (Modsy) are quick, high-signal opening questions.
- **Keepers** are captured early and treated as constraints the design works around.
- **Named blends, not single labels.** Modsy's "Rustic Warmth" read as personal because it was a mix.
- **The deliverable explains itself.** Havenly's designer notes and Modsy's layout notes say *why*.

The generic templates show what to avoid: ticking style names, a long flat list, and logistics before feeling.

## 4. How designers read clients

What they listen and watch for, beyond the literal answer:

- **The trigger and the pain.** What's not working is more actionable than what they want. "We never sit in here"
  says more than "I want it cozy".
- **Feeling words.** Bobby Berk and others refuse to ask "what's your style?"; they ask how the space should make
  you (and guests) feel. A label like "mid-century" means something different to everyone.
- **Where they get animated.** The topics someone lingers on, adds detail to or tells a story about are where to
  dig. That is Maeve's "follow where they bite".
- **What in the image they mean.** Designers always ask what *specifically* draws someone to a picture. One client
  loved a room, it turned out, only for its tile backsplash (Karen Harautuneian, Business of Home).
- **The common thread across images.** Not any one picture, but what repeats (Debbie Travin; Courtney B. Smith asks
  for 30+ images, not all interiors, to build "a filter").
- **Collaborator or delegator.** "Do you want to be part of every choice, or shown a finished idea?" "Do lots of
  options energize or overwhelm you?" (Designers Today). This sets how many choices to show later.
- **Who decides, and do they agree.** Couples often think they agree and don't. Designers have each partner do the
  questionnaire or board separately, then look for shared values (comfort, durability) under surface disagreements
  (DesignDash). Some split authority: one leads function, one leads looks.
- **Budget realism.** Clients often name a number "pulled out of the air". Most budgets are lower than the vision;
  the designer's job is to make trade-offs explicit, not to judge.
- **Personality clues.** Weekend habits, structured vs spontaneous holidays, how they dress, how they talk about
  their past homes. One designer uses the "homework" of collecting images to see how detail-oriented a client is.

## 5. The design brief

The intake produces a **brief**: the document the design is judged against. Common sections (101 Studio, Milanote,
Bonsai, template toolkits):

1. Project background: why now, what to keep, what must change.
2. People and routines: who, ages, pets, accessibility, work, cooking, sleep, guests.
3. Room functions and scenarios: ordinary days and occasional events.
4. Style and references: liked and disliked images, notes on color, texture, light, materials, atmosphere,
   **described in the client's own words**.
5. Technical constraints: light and orientation, noise, renting, building rules.
6. Budget and priorities: a working range, where to invest, what can be phased.
7. Decisions and timeline: who approves, key dates (a baby, a party, a lease).

101 Studio's tip: close the first meeting with a five-part summary (style, goals, must-haves, exclusions, dates and
next step), and confirm it in writing as one shared document. That is our dossier.

## 6. Budget and timeline conversations

- Treat budget as **a design parameter alongside style and function**, not a hurdle (ArchitectGPT). Clients
  collaborate instead of getting defensive.
- Ask for a **range**, plus whether it has give, and whether the outcome or the number matters more (Little Design
  Corner).
- Rapport first. People are honest about money once they trust you.
- Educate with examples, not lectures: "a sofa that lasts 15 years is usually $X–Y".
- Use the **2-of-3 trade-off** (quality, speed, cost) to frame choices. Homebody's `tradeoff` question is this.
- Timeline: ask for **dates that matter** (a move-in, a holiday, guests) rather than "how fast". Dates explain
  urgency and set in-stock vs made-to-order.

## 7. Pitfalls designers avoid

| Pitfall | Why it fails | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Asking "what's your style?" | People don't know the vocabulary, or use it differently. Labels box people in. | Show images; ask how it should feel; name the style for them afterwards. |
| A long form before any contact | Drop-off; surface answers. | Short, warm conversation; depth later. |
| Only asking about likes | Taste is often clearer as dislikes. | Always offer "never in my home". |
| Taking an image literally | They may love one detail, not the room. | Ask "what is it about this one?" |
| Copying one picture | Feels generic, and impossible on their budget. | Find the thread across many images. |
| Budget avoided or asked too bluntly | Unrealistic plans, or a defensive client. | A range, framed as a design input, after rapport. |
| One partner answers for both | Surprise disagreements later. | Let each person react, or ask "is anyone else deciding?" |
| Ending with a question list | Feels like paperwork. | End with a story read back: "here's what I heard". |
| Compromise as the answer to conflict | "Bland, personality-free spaces neither loves." | Find shared values; give each person a lead area. |
| Theming a meaningful detail | Grandma's butterflies become a butterfly room. | One quiet nod. |
