# People: designing around the person

Last updated: 2026-10-30. Part of `docs/reference/design-principles/` (see `README.md`).

The rest of this library is about rooms; this file is about who lives in them. `../interior-design/intake-process.md`
and `question-bank.md` cover *how to ask*; this file covers *what the answers change* in the design.

---

## 1. Ergonomics: bodies and furniture

### Seating and tables

| Pairing | Surface height | Seat height | Gap (seat to surface) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dining | 28–30" | 17–19" | **10–12"** |
| Counter | 34–36" | 24–26" (24–27") | 10–12" |
| Bar | 40–42" | 28–30" | 10–12" |
| Desk | 28–30" (keyboard 25–27") | 16–21" adjustable | elbows at about 90° over the keyboard |
| Coffee table | within 1–2" of the sofa seat | sofa 16–19" | – |

- **Knee clearance under tables:** at least 27" high, 30" wide and 19" deep for a wheelchair user (ADA 306). Aprons
  steal height: a 30" table with a 3" apron leaves 27".
- **Footrests** on counter and bar stools.

### Sofas and lounge chairs

| Dimension | Typical | Adjust for |
|---|---|---|
| Seat height | 17–19" | **Older adults and people with knee or hip trouble: 18–20" and firm**, with arms to push up from; petite: 15–17" |
| Seat depth (usable) | 20–22" | **Tall people (over about 6'): 22–24"+**; loungers and nappers: deep (24–28") with loose back cushions; petite: ≤ 20" (otherwise feet don't reach the floor) |
| Back height | 30–36" overall | Low backs read modern and open a room; high backs support heads |
| Arm height | 22–26" | |
| Firmness | – | Firm for older adults and posture; soft for lounging |

### Beds
- Top of mattress **about 20–25" from the floor** (knee height when sitting on the edge); higher (25"+) for older
  adults or people with mobility issues, lower for platform and Japandi looks or for kids.
- Nightstand top within ±2" of the mattress top.

### Kitchens and work
- Counters at 36"; a person's comfortable work height is about 4–6" below their elbow. Tall households may want 38–39".
- Upper cabinet bottom 18" above the counter; top shelf within reach (about 72" for an average adult).

### Reach ranges (accessibility)
- Unobstructed forward and side reach: **15–48" above the floor** (ADA 308) for anything that must be used: switches,
  hooks, open storage of daily items, controls.

## 2. Accessibility and universal design

Private homes aren't legally bound by the ADA, but its numbers are the best-documented benchmark. Use them whenever
the intake reveals a wheelchair, walker, cane, low vision, older adults, or someone recovering from surgery.

| Need | Rule |
|---|---|
| Routes | **36" continuous clear width** through the room and to every piece used; 60" passing space where two people meet |
| Turning | **60" diameter circle** (or a T-turn in a 60×60" square) next to the bed, the seating and the desk; 67" covers ~95% of chair users (ICC A117.1-2017) |
| Bed | 36" clear along at least one long side for a transfer; mattress top about 20–23" (seat height of the chair) |
| Tables | Knee clearance as above; pedestal bases or legs at the corners (not aprons or stretchers in the way) |
| Seating | Firm, 18–20" seat with sturdy arms; avoid deep, low, soft sofas as the only seating |
| Rugs | Low pile (≤ 1/2"), with a non-slip pad and taped or beveled edges; **no small throw rugs** (a leading fall risk) |
| Low vision | **≥ 30 LRV points of contrast** between floor and wall, door and wall, furniture and floor; matte finishes (less glare); avoid busy patterns on floors (read as steps or holes) |
| Lighting | More light (2× for older eyes), even and glare-free; switches at room entries; night-path lighting from bed to bathroom |
| Hardware | Lever handles and D-pulls, not knobs |
| Hearing | Soft surfaces (rugs, curtains, upholstered pieces) to reduce echo; seating that lets people see faces |
| Neurodivergent or sensory-sensitive people | Calm, low-pattern, low-clutter schemes; closed storage; dimmable warm light; a quiet retreat zone; predictable layout |

## 3. Lifestyle → design decisions

Each signal the intake hears should change something concrete. (Craft-standards: "a note is a fact and the decision
it drives".)

| Signal | Decisions |
|---|---|
| **Young kids** | Performance or washable fabric (≥ 30k double rubs); rounded corners, no glass tables; anchored tall furniture; closed low storage for toys; a play zone visible from the kitchen; rugs that hide spills (patterned, mid-tone, washable) |
| **Teenagers** | Durable, large seating; a homework surface; charging spots |
| **Dog** | Tight weave, microfiber or protected leather; no loop pile; a washable rug; the dog bed out of walkways; mid-tone fabric near the dog's coat color |
| **Cat** | No loop pile or loose weave (claws); velvet or microfiber; sturdy, scratch-tolerant or deliberately sacrificial scratching surfaces; window perches; high shelves are a feature |
| **Hosts a lot** | Extra seating (ottomans, stools, a bench), a dining table that extends, seating group ≥ 6, durable rug, a bar or landing surfaces |
| **Rarely hosts, lounges** | A deep sofa or sectional, a big ottoman, soft lighting, fewer chairs |
| **Works from home** | A real desk with task light and a good chair; screen not facing a window; a work zone away from the TV's sightline; video background (a tidy shelf or art behind the desk) |
| **Cooks** | Counter landing space, open storage for daily tools, washable surfaces, task light |
| **Reader** | A reading chair with a lamp and side table; bookshelves sized to the collection (about 8–10 books per linear foot) |
| **Plants** | Placement by light (see `lighting.md` §6); waterproof saucers; hanging or stand options |
| **Collector or gatherer** | Display surfaces with negative space; picture ledges; deliberate gallery walls; closed storage for overflow |
| **Renters** | No wall damage: plug-in sconces, leaning art or ledges, removable wallpaper, rugs to cover floors; furniture that moves |
| **Moves often** | Modular or knock-down pieces, standard sizes (a queen bed, an 84" sofa) |
| **Shoes-off household** | Entry bench, closed shoe storage, a rug at the door |
| **Sleeps in by day / shift work** | Blackout lining, dark bedroom palette, quiet textiles |

## 4. Culture and meaning

Designers treat cultural practice as **information about the person**, never a costume (interior-design README: "a
layer on a base style, not a costume"). Ask; don't assume from a name or a place.

- **Vastu Shastra (Indian):** directional guidance for rooms and furniture, e.g. sleeping with the head toward the
  south or east, heavy furniture in the south-west, the prayer or puja corner in the north-east, the entrance kept
  clear and bright, mirrors not facing the bed. Some clients follow it strictly; many follow a few rules. Ask "Are
  there any traditions you follow about where things go?"
- **Feng shui (Chinese):** the "commanding position" (the bed and desk see the door without being in line with it);
  no beds under beams or windows; clutter-free entries; color by element. Overlaps with good sightline practice.
- **Color meanings vary:** white is associated with mourning in parts of East and South Asia; red means luck and
  celebration in Chinese culture but danger or alarm in many Western contexts; green has religious meaning in Islam;
  saffron and marigold are festive and sacred in India. Don't assume a color is neutral in meaning.
- **Practices that change the plan:** shoes off (an entry bench and storage); floor seating and low tables (cushions,
  a low platform, durable rugs); multigenerational households (more seating, accessible paths, a guest bed);
  prayer spaces (a quiet, clean, oriented corner, e.g. toward Mecca for Muslim prayer, or a north-east puja shelf);
  big shared meals (a larger table, extra chairs).
- **Heritage as a nod:** turn a place or heritage into one or two buyable specifics (block-print cushions, a brass
  lamp, a jaali screen, a Danish paper pendant), consistent with the base style.
- **Personal meaning:** "my grandmother loved butterflies" → one considered object (a framed vintage botanical
  print with a butterfly), not a theme (product vision, Taste Builder).

## 5. From brief to choices: how designers decide

Designers turn a brief into decisions in a consistent order. Homebody's model should follow it.

1. **Separate hard constraints from preferences.** Hard: room dimensions, doors and windows, kept items, budget
   ceiling, accessibility needs, pets and kids, rental limits, cultural rules the client follows. Preferences:
   style blend, palette, materials, patterns. Constraints are filters; preferences are scores.
2. **Write the concept** in a sentence and 3 mood words, each tied to a means (craft-standards: "warm = walnut +
   brass + 2700K"). Example: "A calm, low reading room: white oak and oat linen, one rust note, paper light."
3. **Plan function first:** zones, the furniture list (what pieces the activities need), circulation and focal
   point. Plan the layout *before* choosing products, with generic sizes.
4. **Choose the hero piece** (the splurge, the thing that makes the room theirs) and the anchors: sofa or bed,
   rug, main light.
5. **Set the palette roles** (60-30-10) and **material kit** (dominant wood, metal, 3+ textures) around the hero and
   the kept items.
6. **Fill the supporting pieces** to the rules (scale, clearances, lighting layers), at the budget allocation.
7. **Accessorize last:** pillows, throws, art, objects, plants, in odd groups, with accent repeats and one personal nod.
8. **Edit:** remove one thing (often the last accessory added); check negative space.
9. **Explain each choice** in the brief by its reason: function, the person, taste, or a rule.

### Budget allocation (rule of thumb)
**Spend on what you touch and use daily; save on what you look at.** A sofa, mattress, desk chair, dining chairs
and a main rug get the money; side tables, decor and accent chairs can be cheaper. For a living room, the sofa often
takes about 30–40% of the furniture budget, with the rug 10–15% and lighting 10–15%. These are practice heuristics,
not a standard.

### Taste vs function conflicts
Function wins for safety and daily use (pets → no bouclé, even if they love it; offer a bouclé-*look* performance
weave). Taste wins for everything visible that doesn't hurt function. Say the trade-off out loud in the brief.
